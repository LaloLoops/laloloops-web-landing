The problem was not that the agents were bad. The problem was that I had accidentally become middle management for autocomplete.

## The part everyone demos

The demo version of agents is clean. You ask for a feature. The agent writes code. A progress bar happens. Everyone nods like this is a normal way for software to exist now.

The real version is less cinematic.

The agent writes code, yes. It also makes assumptions, forgets context, over-solves tiny problems, under-solves important ones, and occasionally decides that a button needs a new design system because the future must suffer.

None of this means agents are useless. It means they are workers. Useful workers. Fast workers. Workers who need direction.

## The missing layer is management

Prompting is not management. Prompting is assigning a task.

Management is deciding what matters, what can be ignored, where quality needs to be high, where speed is fine, and when a clever solution is just a larger bug wearing nice shoes.

This is the part that surprised me. I expected to spend less time thinking about implementation. I do. But I spend more time thinking about operating rules.

What should the agent read first? What should it never touch? What counts as done? Which tests matter? When should it ask for help? How much autonomy is useful before it becomes expensive theater?

The answers are not glamorous. They are checklists, constraints, review habits, and small rituals. The future arrived and asked for a standup.

## The practical rule

The best agent setups I have used are not the ones with the fanciest prompts. They are the ones with the clearest boundaries.

Give the agent a narrow goal. Give it the files that matter. Tell it what not to change. Make it verify the work. Make it explain the tradeoff. Then review the result like a tired engineer with one coffee left and no patience for vibes.

That sounds boring because it is. Boring is underrated. Boring is where systems become repeatable.

## What this means for builders

Agents do not remove the need for technical judgment. They move judgment earlier and make the consequences faster.

If you are a builder, this is good news. Your taste, architecture sense, and ability to define useful constraints still matter. Probably more than before.

The job is not to become a prompt wizard. The job is to become a better operator of small, strange, very eager teams.

Which is a ridiculous sentence.

Also, apparently, the job.
