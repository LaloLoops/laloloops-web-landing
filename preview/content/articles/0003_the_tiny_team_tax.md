One-person leverage with agents is real. So is the tiny team tax.

## Leverage has a receipt

The useful part of agents is obvious once they start doing real work.

They draft boilerplate. They inspect code. They catch boring mistakes. They generate first passes. They remember the command you were definitely going to remember, until your brain opened seventeen tabs and left the building.

This is real leverage. I am not pretending otherwise.

The weird part is that leverage does not arrive alone. It brings coordination costs, context costs, review costs, and the occasional moment where you ask a machine to make a simple edit and it returns with a new worldview.

Very efficient. Deeply unsettling.

## The tax shows up quietly

At first, the tax feels small.

You write a better prompt. You add instructions. You review the diff. You clarify a requirement. You rerun the tests. You explain that no, the homepage does not need an enterprise-grade state machine.

Individually, these moments are fine. Together, they become an operating model.

This is where a lot of agent workflows get overhyped. The pitch is "one person can now do the work of a team." The more accurate version is "one person can now coordinate a small team if they build enough process to keep the team from enthusiastically walking into traffic."

Less catchy. More useful.

## The answer is not less autonomy

The lazy conclusion is to lock agents down until they are basically autocomplete with a name tag.

That wastes the point.

The better move is scoped autonomy. Let agents run where mistakes are cheap and review is easy. Keep tighter control where product judgment, privacy, architecture, or brand voice matter. Make the handoff explicit. Make the done state boringly clear.

Agents are very good at momentum. Humans still need to aim it.

## The tiny team needs a manager

This is the part I am trying to get better at: not just building with agents, but building the system around them.

Instructions. Templates. Review loops. Local previews. Publishing rules. Privacy boundaries. The unglamorous furniture of getting work out the door without accidentally turning a small website into a haunted process diagram.

The tax is not a reason to avoid agents. It is the price of using them well.

And like every tax, the first step is admitting it exists before it arrives with penalties and a smug PDF.
